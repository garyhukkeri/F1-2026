Leclerc's DNF — Was it Brake Degradation?
Generated: 2026-06-09
Leclerc retired (DNF) on lap 65. The popular explanation is a brake problem. This dataset has no brake-temperature or brake-wear channel — the brake signal is binary (pedal on/off) — so true thermal fade can't be measured directly. What we can do is look for the behavioural fingerprints of failing brakes: brake points creeping earlier, brake zones getting longer, and minimum speeds rising because the car won't slow. The verdict from the green-flag laps: those fingerprints are absent. His braking stayed rock-steady through lap 59.

The Pit-Lane Speeding Storm — Five Drivers, Six 5-Second Penalties
Generated: 2026-06-09
Monaco runs the strictest pit-lane speed limit on the calendar — 60 km/h, versus the usual 80 km/h elsewhere — policed by FIA transponder loops to a fraction of a km/h. In this race the loops caught Hamilton, Russell, Colapinto, Piastri and Gasly over the limit. Gasly was caught twice (once in green-flag running, once under the Safety Car), so five drivers collected six separate 5-second penalties. The reason it became so controversial is in the data below: the offences clustered almost entirely in the chaotic pit rush triggered by the late Safety Cars and the red flag, and the punishment was wildly uneven — Hamilton, Gasly and Piastri still made the podium places, while Russell's slip cascaded into a full drive-through and a points-less afternoon.

Shaded bands mark the two Safety Car periods and the red flag (laps 60–68). Note how Gasly's second offence and the bulk of the late drama land squarely inside the Safety Car window, and how Russell's case escalates into a drive-through for serving his penalty incorrectly.

2 · Why it happened — pit-lane congestion vs penalties

Bars show how many cars used the pit lane on each lap of the race. Pit traffic is a trickle for the first 58 laps, then explodes once the Safety Cars and red flag arrive: 10 stops on lap 59, then 9, 9 and a remarkable 16 on lap 68 as the whole field funnels through the 60 km/h lane at once. The red markers show when each speeding penalty was issued — they pile up exactly where the pit lane is most congested. A narrow, downhill-then-uphill pit lane, a 60 km/h ceiling, and the entire grid arriving together is the recipe that caught five drivers.

3 · The uneven cost — same crime, very different sentence

Every driver here committed the same offence and took the same base 5-second hit, yet the outcomes could not be more different. Because the Safety Cars bunched the field, Hamilton, Gasly and Piastri served their penalties and still finished P2, P3 and P5. Russell, by contrast, served his incorrectly, was handed a drive-through on top, and fell to P14; Colapinto's pair of issues left him P15. That disproportion — a time-loss penalty being nearly free for the leaders but race-ruining for the midfield — is the heart of the controversy.
Did They Actually Speed — and By How Much?
Generated: 2026-06-09
The drivers' defence — and Alpine's basis for requesting an FIA Right of Review over Gasly's penalty — was that they never truly breached 60 km/h, and that Monaco's curved pit exit and the placement of the timing-loop / exit line made the measurement unfair. We can test that against the on-car telemetry. The pit-lane "controlled zone" shows up as the limiter plateau: a long, flat run where the throttle climbs but the speed stays pinned (Monaco's slow corners are too brief to confuse it). We read the held limiter speed and the in-zone peak for every pit passage of the five drivers. Caveat: this is GPS-derived speed rounded to whole km/h (≈ ±1), not the FIA's transponder-loop data — which is precisely why a 1 km/h argument is even possible.
The short answer. On the green-flag stops they were actually penalised for, all five drivers held 59–60 km/h — sitting right on the limit, with only isolated single-sample blips of 61–63 km/h that are inside GPS rounding noise. By this data they did not meaningfully exceed 60 on those stops. The only sustained breaches in the entire race (held 63–66 km/h, ~3–6 over) came on Safety-Car / red-flag pit exits — exactly the curved, accelerating section the drivers complained about, where releasing the limiter a fraction early puts the car over the line right where the loop measures.
